*By Divyendu Bhatt \| 30 September 2026*

A cancer diagnosis brings two shocks. The first is medical: the fear of losing someone you love. The second arrives with the prescription: the fear that keeping them alive may cost more than your family can afford.

Savings meant for retirement become treatment funds. Families may borrow, sell land or pledge jewellery. Each prescription brings another calculation. At precisely the moment they need clarity and care, they must negotiate a market they barely understand.

On 29 September 2026, the Supreme Court questioned medicine markups and hospital pharmacy practices. The Centre sought time for consultations; the next hearing was listed for 12 October. The case remains under consideration.

For a court, the next date allows a response to be prepared. For a family, it may mean another medicine bill before that response arrives. Consultation is necessary. So is urgency.

One example discussed in the proceedings captures the concern: a cancer medicine with a price to retailer, or PTR, of approximately **₹2,700** and a maximum retail price of **₹27,000**. That is ten times the retailer price a **900% markup over PTR**, with a gap of ₹24,300.

PTR is not manufacturing cost, and the entire difference cannot automatically be called net profit. Yet a spread of this size demands a transparent explanation. The patient should be able to understand what is being charged, who receives it and why.

Under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013, scheduled formulations come within the ceiling-price framework. Non-scheduled medicines generally have greater freedom in setting their initial prices, while subsequent MRP increases are limited to 10% over the preceding twelve months. Specific government interventions can impose additional controls.

Petitioner Kishan Chand Jain reportedly submitted that around **82% of medicines** fall outside the scheduled category. That is a submission made to the court, not an independently established finding by the bench.

The distinction exposes a basic weakness: restricting future increases does little to correct an excessive starting price. A ceiling on how quickly a bill rises cannot, by itself, establish whether the original bill was reasonable.

Existing intervention offers a precedent. In 2019, the NPPA introduced trade-margin controls for 42 selected non-scheduled anti-cancer medicines, with a 30% trade-margin cap under that framework. The issue now is how to make protection broader, consistent and effective throughout the supply chain.

The inquiry must follow the money. Solicitor General Tushar Mehta reportedly indicated that pharmaceutical manufacturers were not necessarily the principal beneficiaries of these spreads, and that private hospitals could capture the margins. That calls for scrutiny of each stage between supply and patient billing.

The bench’s reported remark was sharp: **“Corporate hospitals are industries. It is not a service at all.”**

Hospitals need skilled staff, reliable equipment, safe storage and sustainable finances. Those costs deserve honest pricing. But a patient’s dependence on treatment must never become permission to charge an unexplained premium. Any restriction on medicines bought outside should have a documented clinical or quality justification and a fair, transparent process.

Inflated prices also damage trust. The court raised the example of a chemist offering a ₹27,000-MRP medicine for ₹3,000: a frightened patient might suspect that the cheaper medicine is counterfeit.

That is a cruel trap. The price tag begins to stand in for proof of quality. Patients need verifiable sourcing, appropriate storage and reliable quality assurance, so that an affordable medicine does not become an object of suspicion merely because it costs less.

The court also flagged the taxpayer’s stake when treatment is publicly funded.

For schemes such as Ayushman Bharat, scrutiny should follow procurement, billing and the actual reimbursement rules. An allegation of inflated pricing must be traced through real claims and payments. Public funds intended to expand access deserve protection from overbilling, just as families deserve protection from unjustified charges.

The court is asking whether the 16% retailer margin used in scheduled-price calculations should apply more widely. **It has not yet imposed a universal cap.**

A workable reform must define the price on which a margin is calculated, account for legitimate distribution costs and prevent manipulation of that base. A percentage alone will achieve little if the price beneath it remains opaque. Affordability, quality and dependable supply must be tested together.

The NPPA should enforce applicable controls, publish accessible price information and make complaints produce timely answers. Hospital bills should identify the medicine, quantity, unit price and applicable ceiling. Patients should be told about affordable, clinically appropriate options before purchase.

Clear generic-name prescribing, where clinically appropriate, should be backed by assured quality and the treating clinician’s guidance on substitution. Jan Aushadhi and AMRIT pharmacies already form part of the government’s affordability response. Their practical value should be judged by whether patients can reliably obtain the medicines they need.

The next hearing should bring a concrete government response: what will change, who will enforce it and how patients will experience relief. A family cannot pay its next prescription bill with a promise that the problem is under consideration.

“Tarikh par tarikh” resonates because it expresses the exhaustion of waiting. In a cancer household, waiting is measured in anxiety, depleted savings and the next treatment appointment.

Justice delayed can deny justice. Pricing reform delayed can put treatment beyond reach.

**The court’s next date is 12 October. The family’s next bill may arrive sooner.**

A medical diagnosis must never become a financial death warrant.
