Friday **\#Thought**

The **\#AI** era has created an abundance of ideas, tools and possibilities.

The leadership challenge is no longer generating ideas. It is deciding:

Which ideas deserve attention?

Which can create measurable business value?

Which are simply distractions?

I do not try to absorb everything. My role is changing from being a repository of answers to becoming a sense-maker and value orchestrator.

Here is how I manage the chaos:

1\. **Classify the ideas**

I organize incoming ideas into strategic themes:

-Business growth

-Customer experience

-Operational efficiency

-Security and resilience

-Regulatory value

This converts a flood of disconnected thoughts into a manageable opportunity portfolio.

**2. Apply a value filter For every significant idea**, I ask:

-What problem does it solve?

-Who benefits from it?

-Is it aligned with our strategy?

-What risks does it introduce?

-Can we validate it quickly?

-What measurable value could it deliver?

**3. Separate exploration from commitment**

Promising ideas enter small, time-bound experiments.

We validate the problem, technical feasibility, security implications and potential business value before allocating significant resources.

**4. Create structure around execution** - Architecture and security review boards, incident forums, technical guilds and retrospectives allow teams to exchange knowledge without creating uncontrolled decision-making.

These mechanisms convert individual ideas into organizational intelligence.

**5. Protect execution from continuous disruption**

Once priorities are agreed, I create a stable execution window while keeping new ideas in a structured discovery pipeline. This allows teams to innovate without losing delivery discipline.

**What has noticeably changed in 2026?**

1)The discovery phase has become much shorter.

2)Proofs of concept are being completed faster.

3)Business teams are responding more quickly.

4)Communication has improved.

Most importantly, people are arriving prepared. They have researched the subject, structured their requirements and developed an initial point of view.

Thank you, ChatGPT. 😊

**\#Securityawareness** has also reached a level I spent years trying to create. As a **\#CISO**, one of my biggest historical pain points convincing people that security matters is finally becoming smaller.

But AI has created another **\#leadershipchallenge**:

Suddenly, everybody is an **\#expert**.

People sometimes believe that an AI-generated answer gives them more authority than the subject-matter expert sitting across the table.

**\#Listening** capability is declining.

AI is increasingly being used to argue, defend predetermined positions and prove someone wrong instead of helping people understand one another and solve the problem together.

But **\#AI**-generated knowledge is not the same as experience. It may provide information, but it does not automatically provide context, accountability, judgement or wisdom.

The purpose of AI should not be to help us win every <a href="http://argument.It" target="_blank">argument.It</a> should help us ask better questions, challenge our assumptions, listen more intelligently and make better collective decisions.

My role as a leader is therefore not to know everything or chase every new AI development. It is to create **\#clarity** in ambiguity, connect technology with business outcomes, manage risk and enable teams to execute the few ideas that matter most.

Are we using AI to improve our thinking or merely to strengthen what we already believe?
