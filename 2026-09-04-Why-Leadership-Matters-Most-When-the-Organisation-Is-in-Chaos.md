# Why Leadership Matters Most When the Organisation Is in Chaos

# Why Leadership Matters Most When the Organisation Is in Chaos

Created on 2026-09-04 02:00

<div>

I have often entered organisations at moments when operational stability was already under significant pressure.

The circumstances were different, but the patterns were often similar:

1.  Legal or regulatory issues

2.  M&A-related uncertainty

3.  IPO or listing pressure

4.  Leadership exits

5.  Restructuring and changing reporting lines

6.  Priorities being rewritten while teams were still trying to deliver yesterday’s commitments

A reporting manager changes.

Then the manager above them changes.

People leave.

Rumours begin.

Priorities become unclear.

Teams start asking themselves what will happen next.

In such situations, leadership is not about creating more urgency.

**It is about creating order.**

Over the years, I have developed a relatively simple approach to leading teams through operational chaos.

### 1. Stabilize Before You Optimize

When everything appears urgent, the first responsibility of a leader is to distinguish between noise and genuine business risk.

What can materially damage:

-   the business,

-   the customer,

-   employees,

-   security,

-   regulatory commitments,

-   compliance,

-   or operational continuity?

Not every fire deserves the same response.

The first job is therefore not transformation.

It is stabilization.

Only after stability starts returning should optimization become the priority.

### 2. Create One Version of Operational Truth

Rumours grow fastest when facts are missing.

During turbulent periods, I have always believed in being as transparent as possible with the team.

No encouragement of rumours.

No support for rumour-mongering.

No unnecessary speculation.

As information becomes available from senior management, the team should be kept appropriately informed.

I normally try to create one clear operational view around six questions:

**What is happening?**

**What is impacted?**

**Who owns it?**

**What decision is required?**

**What is the deadline?**

**What happens if we do nothing?**

Creating that single version of operational truth removes a surprising amount of chaos.

People may not always like the answer.

But uncertainty is usually more damaging than an uncomfortable fact.

### 3. Protect the Team From Organisational Noise

During leadership turbulence, employees naturally begin worrying about restructures, reporting lines, internal politics, job security and what senior leadership changes may mean for them.

My team should not have to interpret every movement happening at the executive level.

That is part of my responsibility as a leader.

I try to translate organisational uncertainty into something much simpler:

**This is what we know.**

**This is what we do not know.**

**This is what we are responsible for.**

**This is what we are going to deliver.**

People perform better when the boundaries of responsibility are clear.

### 4. Reduce the Number of Priorities

A team under sustained pressure cannot successfully execute twenty priorities at the same time.

During turbulent periods, I usually reduce the operating agenda into three categories:

### \#Protect

Protect what cannot be allowed to fail.

### \#Stabilize

Stabilize what is already failing or showing signs of instability.

### \#Improve

Improve only what creates meaningful business leverage.

This forces the organisation to distinguish between what is important and what is merely desirable.

### 5. Increase Leadership Cadence

One of my strongest learnings has been very simple:

**During uncertainty, talk to people more often.**

During stable periods, a weekly governance meeting may be sufficient.

During \#OperationalChaos, leadership visibility has to increase.

That means:

-   shorter reviews,

-   clearer ownership,

-   faster decisions,

-   visible blockers,

-   written commitments,

-   regular communication,

-   and very little ambiguity around accountability.

People should not need three meetings to understand who owns a problem.

And decisions should not disappear into conversations that nobody can later remember.

### 6. Never Transfer Executive Anxiety to the Team

A leader may be dealing with boards, regulators, customers, auditors, investors, legal teams or constantly changing executives.

But the team does not need to inherit that emotional load.

They need:

**Context.**

**Decisions.**

**Direction.**

\#CalmLeadership does not mean pretending that everything is fine.

It means demonstrating that even a difficult situation can be understood, broken down and managed.

A leader can acknowledge the seriousness of a situation without amplifying fear.

That distinction matters.

### 7. Keep People Connected to Purpose

When several people begin leaving an organisation, one question quickly enters the minds of those who remain:

**“Should I also leave?”**

I have learned that motivation during such periods rarely comes from speeches.

It comes from something much more practical.

Give capable people meaningful ownership.

Protect them from unnecessary politics.

Recognise their contribution.

Explain why their work still matters.

Give them visibility into the mission.

Create enough stability that they can focus on doing good work.

People can tolerate uncertainty far better when they still feel that their contribution has purpose.

### 8. Build an Operating System That Survives Leadership Changes

The strongest leadership outcome is not creating a team that becomes dependent on the leader.

It is exactly the opposite.

Processes, decision rights, risk governance, escalation paths, metrics, operational controls and ownership should eventually become strong enough that the organisation continues functioning even when leaders change.

Good \#leadership should reduce organisational dependency on individual personalities.That, to me, is one of the strongest tests of leadership maturity.

I have worked through environments involving organisational restructuring, leadership transitions, M&A, regulatory pressure, legal challenges and major corporate transformation.

One lesson has remained remarkably consistent:

**When the organisation becomes unstable, a disciplined leader becomes part of the stabilizing system.**

Not by controlling everything.

But by creating:

**Clarity where there is confusion.**

**Priority where there is noise.**

**Accountability where there is ambiguity.**

**Confidence where people are beginning to lose it.**

Leadership is relatively easy to demonstrate when the machine is already working.

The real test begins when the machine starts shaking.

Can the team still think clearly?

Can people still trust the direction?

Can decisions still be made?

Can critical operations continue?

Can good people still believe that their work matters?

That is where leadership becomes visible.
